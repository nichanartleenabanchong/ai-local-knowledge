SONGKRAN FESTIVAL (Thai New Year) | ประเพณีสงกรานต์ ปีใหม่ไทย รดน้ำดำหัว ก่อเจดีย์ทราย

Overview
Songkran is the traditional Thai New Year. The word "Songkran" comes from the Sanskrit word "sankranti", which means the passing or movement of the sun from one zodiac sign to the next. Songkran marks the sun entering Aries (Mesha), which was the start of the year in the old solar calendar.

Dates
Songkran is officially celebrated on 13, 14 and 15 April every year. These dates are fixed national public holidays in Thailand.
- 13 April: Wan Maha Songkran (the day the old year passes)
- 14 April: Wan Nao (the "in-between" day)
- 15 April: Wan Thaloeng Sok (the first day of the new era/new year)
Thailand used 1 April as the official New Year until 1940, and changed the official New Year to 1 January from 1941. Songkran stayed as the traditional New Year.

History and legend
The legend of Songkran tells of Kabil Maha Phrom, a god who lost a riddle contest to a wise young man named Thammabal Kumar. Kabil Maha Phrom had to cut off his own head. Because his head was so powerful that it would cause disaster if it touched the ground, burned fire in the air, or dried up the sea, his seven daughters take turns carrying the head in a procession each year. Each daughter is called "Nang Songkran" (Songkran Lady). Which daughter carries the head depends on the day of the week that Songkran begins.

In December 2023, UNESCO added "Songkran in Thailand, traditional Thai New Year festival" to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Rituals and customs
- Rot nam dam hua: younger people pour scented water (often with jasmine or nam ob Thai) over the hands of parents, elders and respected people to show respect and ask for blessings.
- Song nam phra: bathing Buddha images with scented water.
- Tham bun (making merit): giving food to monks in the morning and going to the temple.
- Khon sai khao wat: carrying sand to the temple to build sand chedis (sand pagodas). This replaces the sand people carried away on their feet during the year.
- Releasing birds and fish to make merit.
- Cleaning the house to welcome the new year.
- Splashing water on each other in the streets. Water is a symbol of washing away bad luck and cooling off in the hottest month of the year.
- Dinsor pong (white clay/powder) is sometimes put on people's faces as a blessing.

Regional differences
- North (Lanna), e.g. Chiang Mai: Songkran is called "Pi Mai Mueang". The days have local names: Wan Sangkhan Long (13 April, people clean houses and throw away bad luck), Wan Nao (14 April, people build sand chedis and should not say bad words), Wan Phaya Wan (15 April, merit-making and dam hua to elders), and Wan Pak Pi (16 April, paying respect to the town's guardian spirits and elders).
- Northeast (Isan): Songkran is often called "Bun Duean Ha" (merit-making of the fifth lunar month) or "Trut Songkran". Khon Kaen is famous for its Songkran on Khao Niao Road.
- Central: Phra Pradaeng in Samut Prakan has a Mon-style Songkran with Mon traditions such as the Hong-Thong flag procession and a beauty pageant. It is usually held about a week after 13 April.
- Eastern: Pattaya (Chonburi) holds "Wan Lai", a local water festival held a few days after the main Songkran, usually around 18-19 April.
- Southern: The 14 April day is also called "Wan Nao". Many southern families focus on merit-making and paying respect to elders, and some areas believe the old year's guardian god returns to heaven during the Songkran days.
