# Loy Krathong (ลอยกระทง)

Loy Krathong is held on the full moon night of the 12th month of the Thai lunar calendar (วันเพ็ญเดือนสิบสอง), which usually falls in November. People float small decorated vessels called krathong on rivers, canals and ponds.

## Purpose of floating a krathong
Floating a krathong pays respect to the water goddess Phra Mae Khongkha (พระแม่คงคา), gives thanks for the water that sustains life, and asks forgiveness for polluting or misusing it. Many people also believe that floating the krathong lets go of grudges, anger and bad luck. People often put a coin, incense sticks, a candle and flowers in the krathong, and some add a lock of hair or a fingernail clipping as a symbol of letting go of misfortune.

## History
A popular legend ties the origin of Loy Krathong to the Sukhothai period (13th-15th century). According to this tradition, Nang Noppamas (นางนพมาศ), a consort of King Loethai, made the first decorated krathong in the shape of a lotus. Modern historians note that the story comes from a later text, so the exact origin of the festival is uncertain. The festival was originally connected with Brahmin ceremonies honoring river spirits and was later combined with Buddhist practice, such as paying respect to the Buddha's footprint at the Namtha River in India.

## Krathong materials
Traditional krathongs are made from banana trunk slices, banana leaves and flowers. Since the 1990s, environmental campaigns in Thailand encourage biodegradable materials and discourage foam krathongs.

## Regional differences
In central Thailand, the festival focuses on floating krathongs at rivers and parks. In Sukhothai, the Loy Krathong and Candle Festival is held at Sukhothai Historical Park with light shows and parades. In Chiang Mai and the northern Lanna region, the festival coincides with Yi Peng, which is known for releasing sky lanterns (khom loi).
